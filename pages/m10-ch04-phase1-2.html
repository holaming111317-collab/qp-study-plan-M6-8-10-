M10 Chapter 4: The Law of Tort and Negligence — Phase 1 速查表 + Phase 2 閃卡

Phase 1 速查表

Learning Objectives

  1. Analyse the legal meaning of tort and distinguish the three types of tortious liability (intentional tort, negligence, strict liability).
  2. Apply the three elements of negligence — duty of care, breach of duty, and causation/remoteness of damage — to novel fact patterns using the IRAC method.
  3. Evaluate the availability of defences (contributory negligence, voluntary assumption of risk) and statutory limits on excluding liability.
  4. Assess the tortious liability of professionals (accountants and auditors) for negligent misstatement to clients and third parties.
  5. Distinguish the special liability regimes: employers' liability, vicarious liability, occupiers' liability and defamation.

必背考點

A. Relevant Ordinances (Cap. + section numbers)

Ordinance Provision Rule
Limitation Ordinance (Cap. 347) s.4(1) General limitation period for actions in tort: 6 years
Limitation Ordinance (Cap. 347) s.27 Personal injuries: 3 years; fatal cases: 3 years from date of death or personal representative's knowledge of death, whichever is later
Law Amendment and Reform (Consolidation) Ordinance (Cap. 23) ('LARCO') s.21(1) Contributory negligence does not defeat the claim, but damages are reduced as the court thinks just and equitable
LARCO (Cap. 23) s.21(2) Court must first find and record the total damages that would have been recoverable, then reduce by apportionment
Companies Ordinance (Cap. 622) s.415(2) Any provision exempting an auditor from liability for negligence/default/breach of duty/breach of trust in performing audit duties is void
Companies Ordinance (Cap. 622) s.415(3) Any provision by which the company (directly or indirectly) indemnifies an auditor against such liability is void
Control of Exemption Clauses Ordinance (Cap. 71) ('CECO') s.7(a) Cannot exclude or restrict liability for death or personal injury resulting from negligence
CECO (Cap. 71) s.7(b) For other loss/damage, exclusion only valid if the term satisfies the reasonableness requirement
CECO (Cap. 71) s.7(c) Agreement to / awareness of an exemption clause is not of itself voluntary acceptance of risk
CECO (Cap. 71) s.2(2) ss.7–12 apply only to business liability (subject to s.11(4))
Factories and Industrial Undertakings Ordinance (Cap. 59) ('FIUO') s.6A Proprietor of an industrial undertaking must ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health and safety at work of all employees
FIUO (Cap. 59) s.18 Onus on the proprietor to prove it was not practicable/reasonably practicable to do more, or that all reasonable steps were taken
Occupational Safety and Health Ordinance (Cap. 509) — Statutory duties owed by employer to employees (may be pleaded together with common law safe system of work)
Employees' Compensation Ordinance (Cap. 282) — Statutory compensation claim is a separate action from a common law negligence claim; no double recovery
Occupiers Liability Ordinance (Cap. 314) ('OLO') s.3 Occupier owes a common duty of care — visitors reasonably safe in using the premises
OLO (Cap. 314) s.2(2), s.2(3) Occupier = person with occupancy and control (not ownership) over premises, including fixed/movable structures, vessels, vehicles, aircraft
OLO (Cap. 314) s.4 Landlord may assign duty to tenant, who then becomes liable as occupier
OLO (Cap. 314) s.3(2) Common duty of care defined: reasonable care that the visitor (not the premises) is reasonably safe for permitted purposes
OLO (Cap. 314) s.3(3)(a) Occupier must expect children to be less careful than adults (allurement/trap)
OLO (Cap. 314) s.3(3)(b) Occupier may expect a skilled visitor to guard against special risks ordinarily incident to his calling (not defects in the premises)
OLO (Cap. 314) s.3(4)(a) A warning does not absolve the occupier unless in all the circumstances it was enough to enable the visitor to be reasonably safe
OLO (Cap. 314) s.3(4)(b) Occupier not answerable for faulty work of a competent independent contractor if he acted reasonably in selecting and checking
OLO (Cap. 314) s.3(5) No obligation for risks willingly accepted by the visitor (volenti)
OLO (Cap. 314) s.3(1) Occupier may extend/restrict/exclude liability by agreement, subject to CECO and other restrictions
Defamation Ordinance (Cap. 21) s.26 Defence of justification does not fail merely because not every charge is proved true, if unproved charges do not materially injure reputation
Hong Kong Bill of Rights Ordinance (Cap. 383) s.8, Art. 16 Defamation law must be read in light of freedom of expression

B. Leading Cases (full citation as given in the LP)

Duty of care - Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) AC 562 — the neighbour principle: reasonable care to avoid acts/omissions you can reasonably foresee would injure persons so closely and directly affected that you ought to have them in contemplation. Manufacturer owed duty to ultimate consumer (snail in ginger-beer). - Anns v Merton London Borough Council — two-fold test (proximity + policy negating the duty); later criticised. - Yuen Kun Yeu v Attorney General of Hong Kong (1988) AC 175 PC — Privy Council: Anns first stage too ambiguous; foreseeability of harm must not be neglected. - Caparo Industries plc v Dickman (1990) 2 AC 605 — three-fold test: (1) foreseeability of damage; (2) close and direct relationship of proximity; (3) fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty. Auditor owes duty to shareholders as a body, not to non-shareholders contemplating investment. - Palsgraf v Long Island Railroad Co (1928) 248 NY 339 — no duty to a plaintiff whose injury was unforeseeable to a person of ordinary vigilance. - Bourhill v Young (1943) AC 92 — plaintiff 45–50 feet away, outside the range of foreseeable harm; no liability for nervous shock. - Barrett v Enfield London Borough Council (2001) 2 AC 550 — policy balancing: promoting individual autonomy, preventing indeterminate liability, protecting the vulnerable.

Breach / standard of care - Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co (1856) 11 Exch 781 — the reasonable person test (objective). - Roe v Minister of Health (1954) 2 QB 66 — standard assessed by knowledge at the time of the incident ("must not look at the 1947 accident with 1954 spectacles" — Denning LJ). - Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee (1957) 1 WLR 582 — Bolam test: not negligent if acting in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of skilled professionals; inexperience is no defence — the test focuses on the activity, not the person. - Ho Ying Wai v Keliston Marine (Far East) Ltd (2003) 1 HKLRD 343 — GP who accepted a commercial-diver fitness retainer was expected to acquire the competency of a reasonably competent practitioner in that field; liable. - Bolton v Stone (1951) AC 850 — very low likelihood of risk + high cost of prevention → no breach (cricket ball). - Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951) AC 367 — greater seriousness of potential harm (one-eyed worker) → higher standard; goggles should have been provided. - Latimer v A.E.C. Ltd (1953) AC 643 — employer not required to eliminate risk at disproportionate cost (sawdust on flooded floor sufficient; no duty to close factory). - Rimmer v Liverpool City Council (1984) 1 All ER 930 — codes of practice alert the defendant to appropriate standards; failure to follow may constitute breach.

Causation / remoteness - 'But for' test — plaintiff must prove on the balance of probabilities that but for the defendant's act/omission, the injury would not have occurred. - Barnett v Chelsea Hospital Management Committee (1969) 1 QB 428 — hospital negligent, but victim would have died anyway → causation failed. - McGhee v National Coal Board (1973) 1 WLR 1 — where precise cause is unknown, material contribution to the risk of injury suffices. - Thin skull rule — defendant takes the victim as he finds him: Smith v Leech Brain & Co — liable for full extent (death from cancer triggered by foreseeable burn). - Novus actus interveniens — an intervening act breaks the chain of causation only if it was not reasonably foreseeable to the defendant: Hui Kay Cheong, Herman v Chi Wo Properties Ltd (1992) HKDCLR 51 (foreseeable robbery did NOT break the chain) vs Leung Sin Fai v Perfect World Co Ltd (1998) 2 HKLRD 115 (unforeseeable inflammable liquid DID break the chain). - Res ipsa loquitur — Sanfield Building Contractors Ltd v Li Kai Cheong (2003) 3 HKLRD 48 (CFA): accident of unknown cause that would not normally happen without negligence, where defendant controlled the object/activity → inference of negligence unless defendant offers an acceptable explanation consistent with reasonable care (essence per Lord Radcliffe in Barkway v South Wales Transport Co Ltd (1950) 1 All ER 392).

Defences to negligence - Contributory negligence — onus on the defendant; partial defence leading to apportionment under s.21 LARCO (Cap. 23). Chan Wing Kin & Anor v Fonnie Co (1983) HKLR 102 — pregnant front-seat passenger not wearing seat belt NOT contributory negligent in the circumstances (fault, not mere conduct, must cause the injury). - Volenti non fit injuria — complete defence; requires proof of negligence + plaintiff's full knowledge of the nature and extent of the risk + consent (explicit or implied agreement to waive liability); mere knowledge is insufficient. Simms v Leigh Rugby Football Club (1969) 2 All ER 923 — rugby player willingly accepted risks of a compliant ground.

Accountants/auditors & negligent misstatement - Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd (1964) AC 465 — negligent misrepresentation actionable only where a 'special relationship' exists (assumption of responsibility, equivalent to contract; relationships such as solicitor/client, banker/customer); disclaimer 'without responsibility' negated liability. - Caparo Industries plc v Dickman (1990) 2 AC 605 — auditor's duty limited to shareholders' class rights, not potential investors. - James McNaughton Paper Group Ltd v Hicks Anderson & Co (1991) 2 QB 113 — six criteria for duty to a third party: (1) purpose for which the statement was made; (2) purpose for which it was communicated; (3) relationship between advisor, advisee and third party; (4) size of the class to which the advisee belongs; (5) advisor's knowledge of the purpose; (6) reliance by the advisee. Accountant not liable to prospective purchaser who relied on draft accounts without consulting his own accountant.

Employers' liability / vicarious liability - McDermid v Nash Dredging & Reclamation Co Ltd (1987) AC 906 — employer's duty is personal and non-delegable (delegation to a competent person is no defence). Four heads: safe place of work; competent employees + supervision; adequate plant and appliances; safe system of work. - Davie v New Merton Board Mills (1959) 1 All ER 346 / Pearce v Round Oak Steel Works Ltd (1969) 1 WLR 595 — duty discharged by buying from reputable supplier + reasonable inspection; latent defect not discoverable by reasonable care excuses. - Yuen Chung Shun v On Ascent Ltd (2016) 4 HKLRD 250 — complete failure of supervision → employer liable (with employee's contributory negligence). - Cathay Pacific Airways Ltd v Wong Sau Lai (2006) 2 HKLRD 586 — unsafe system of work (no stopping mechanism on drawer); foreseeable momentary lapse under pressure → employer liable. - Vicarious liability (strict liability for employee's torts in the course of employment): Dubai Aluminium Co Ltd v Salaam (2003) 2 AC 366 — employer's innocence irrelevant once employee's liability established. Salmond test (authorised wrongful act, or unauthorised mode of an authorised act); Close Connection Test from Lister v Hesley Hall Ltd (2002) 1 AC 215; applied in HK in Ming An Insurance Co (HK) Ltd v Ritz Carlton Ltd (2002) 3 HKLRD 844 (doorman's unauthorised food run in limousine still within course of employment).

Occupiers' liability - Chu Wo Heung & Anor v Hui Lai Wa (2002) 3 HKLRD 209 — building manager with control over external wall via DMC was an occupier of the awning; control, not ownership. - Cooke v Midland Great Western Rly of Ireland (1909) AC 229 — unlocked turntable = allurement; occupier liable to child trespassers it knew played there. Contrast Latham v R Johnson & Nephew Ltd (1913) 1 KB 398 — ordinary heaps of stones, no trap/allurement → not liable. - Wood v Wah Tung (E&M) Ltd (2000) HKEC 277 — frequent flooding/power failure → general safety reminder inadequate; specific warnings required. - Roles v Nathan (1963) 1 WLR 1117 — chimney sweeps warned of gas risk; risk incidental to their calling → occupier not liable (s.3(3)(b) OLO equivalent). - Tomlinson v Congleton BC (2004) 1 AC 46 — no duty to warn of obvious dangers. - Luen Hing Fat Coating & Finishing Factory Ltd v Waan Chuen Ming (2011) 2 HKLRD 223 — occupier liable for own negligence in failing to engage/supervise a competent independent contractor. - Trespassers: occupier owes no duty of reasonable care, even for concealed danger, unless harm deliberately intended (common law; OLO does not govern trespassers).

Defamation - Elements: (1) defamatory statement (lowers the person in the estimation of right-thinking members of society / hatred, contempt or ridicule); (2) reference to the plaintiff (a purely personal action; a corporation may sue for statements disparaging its goodwill — Tung Lo Court (IO) v Tsui Wai Yip (2015) 4 HKLRD 397); (3) publication to a third party (not the defendant's spouse). - Libel = permanent form; slander = transient form. Defendant's intention is irrelevant; natural and ordinary meaning to ordinary reasonable viewers; legal innuendo must be pleaded separately. - Cheung Ng Sheong Steven v Eastweek Publisher Ltd (1995) 3 HKC 601 — article attacking professor's discharge of duties = libel. - Charles Sin Cho Chiu v Tin Tin Publication Development Ltd HCA 6662/1997 — group reference ('7 Honourable Men', 'tainted elements') can identify an individual plaintiff. - Defences: justification (substantial truth of the imputation; s.26 Defamation Ordinance (Cap. 21)); fair comment (public interest + recognisable comment + based on true fact + honest opinion; defeated by malice); absolute privilege (judicial/quasi-judicial proceedings, public officers, Legislative Council); qualified privilege (duty/interest to communicate; lost if malicious); consent (must not exceed scope given). - Remedies: damages (nominal / general compensatory / aggravated / exemplary / special) and injunction.

C. IRAC Application Points (duty → breach → causation → remoteness → defences)

  • Issue: identify which tort and which relationship (driver/passenger, professional/client, employer/employee, occupier/visitor, publisher/subject). Do not confuse negligent misstatement with ordinary negligence.
  • Rule: state the correct test with authority — Caparo three-fold test for duty; Bolam test for professional standard; but-for test for factual causation; s.21 LARCO for apportionment; s.3 OLO for occupiers.
  • Application: 1. Duty of care — run all three Caparo limbs in order: foreseeable? proximity? fair, just and reasonable? For pure economic loss or psychiatric harm, policy (indeterminate liability) usually narrows the duty. For statements, add Hedley Byrne 'special relationship' / James McNaughton six criteria. 2. Breach — objective reasonable person; adjust for skill (professionals held to their profession's standard; inexperience no defence); weigh likelihood (Bolton v Stone), seriousness (Paris v Stepney), cost of avoidance (Latimer), codes of practice (Rimmer). 3. Causation — but-for first (Barnett); if scientifically uncertain, material contribution (McGhee); if the accident's cause is unknown but speaks of negligence, res ipsa loquitur (Sanfield). 4. Remoteness — foreseeable type of injury → liable for full extent (thin skull, Smith v Leech Brain); unforeseeable intervening act breaks the chain (novus actus); foreseeable third-party crime does not (Hui Kay Cheong). 5. Defences — contributory negligence (apportionment; onus on defendant) vs volenti (complete bar; needs full knowledge + consent); check statutory restrictions on exclusion clauses (CECO s.7; CO s.415(2)/(3)).
  • Conclusion: give a decisive one-sided answer (e.g. "Therefore, X is liable in negligence, subject to a reduction for contributory negligence") — never sit on the fence when facts are clear.

易混淆概念對比

Table 1: Negligence (ordinary) vs Negligent Misstatement

比較點 Negligence (ordinary negligence) Negligent Misstatement / Misrepresentation
Nature of wrong Careless act or omission causing physical injury or property damage Careless statement/advice relied upon, causing (usually) pure economic loss
Basis of duty Neighbour principle / Caparo three-fold test (foreseeability + proximity + fair, just and reasonable) Caparo test plus a 'special relationship' with assumption of responsibility — Hedley Byrne v Heller (1964) AC 465
Duty to third parties Proximity judged by physical closeness, cause and effect, or control Only if James McNaughton six criteria support it (purpose, communication, relationship, class size, knowledge, reliance); auditor's duty stops at shareholders — Caparo
Typical claimant Anyone foreseeably injured by the act Client or a third party the maker knew would rely on the statement
Effect of disclaimer Cannot exclude death/personal injury liability at all (CECO s.7(a)) An express 'without responsibility' disclaimer can negate assumption of responsibility (Hedley Byrne)

點樣分辨：見到「講錯嘢／出錯 report 令人蝕錢」→ 一定諗 negligent misstatement，第一步係搵 special relationship / assumption of responsibility，唔好直接套 neighbour principle。考試伏位：Jun 2025 Panelists' Report 明確指出考生將 negligent misstatement 當普通 negligence 答，直接跌晒 application 分。留意純經濟損失（pure economic loss）本身係 policy 上收緊 duty 嘅訊號（preventing indeterminate liability）。

Statutory basis s.21 LARCO (Cap. 23) Common law; OLO s.3(5) for occupiers

What must be proved Plaintiff's fault contributed to his own injury (plaintiff owes no duty to defendant); onus on defendant Defendant was negligent and plaintiff had full knowledge of the nature and extent of the risk and consented (explicit/implied agreement to waive liability)

Duty of care 謹慎責任：法律要求你對「可預見會受你行為影響嘅人」保持合理小心 Under the Caparo three-fold test, a duty of care arises only where damage is foreseeable, proximity exists, and it is fair, just and reasonable to impose it.
Proximity 接近性：原告同被告之間「夠近」嘅關係，可以係物理上、因果上或管理控制上 Proximity may be established by physical closeness, by cause and effect, or by the defendant's control over the relevant activity.
Novus actus interveniens 介入事件：之後出現嘅獨立新原因，如果被告預見唔到，就會斬斷因果鏈 The surgeon's unforeseeable negligence was a novus actus interveniens that broke the chain of causation between the driver's breach and the patient's death.
Res ipsa loquitur 事實自證：意外本身已說明有疏忽，除非被告提出合理解釋 Where a scaffold of a type that would not normally topple fell without explanation, res ipsa loquitur allowed the court to infer the contractor's negligence (Sanfield).
Volenti non fit injuria 自願承受風險：完全抗辯，但必須證明受害人完全知情兼同意 The defence of volenti non fit injuria failed because mere knowledge of the risk did not amount to consent to waive liability.
Contributory negligence 與有過失：受害人自己都有份造成傷害，賠償按比例扣減 Under s.21 of the LARCO (Cap. 23), the passenger's damages were reduced by 25% for his contributory negligence in not wearing a seat belt.
Vicarious liability 僱主轉承責任：僱員喺受僱期間犯侵權，僱主即使無過失都要孭 Under the Close Connection Test in Lister v Hesley Hall, the employer was vicariously liable because the employee's tort was closely connected with his employment.
