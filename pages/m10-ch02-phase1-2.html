M10 Chapter 2: Principles of Contract and Consumer Law in Hong Kong (Part 1) — Phase 1 速查表 + Phase 2 閃卡

Phase 1 速查表

Learning Objectives

  1. Analyse the essential elements in the formation of a contract (offer, acceptance, capacity, intention to create legal relations, consideration) and apply them to practical business scenarios.
  2. Distinguish offers from invitations to treat, and apply the communication rules of acceptance (including the postal rule and its exceptions).
  3. Evaluate the enforceability of promises through the doctrine of consideration and promissory estoppel.
  4. Apply the rules on express and implied terms, and assess the validity of exemption clauses under the Control of Exemption Clauses Ordinance (Cap.71) and related statutes.
  5. Advise on third-party rights under the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Ordinance (Cap.623) and on unconscionable consumer contracts under the Unconscionable Contracts Ordinance (Cap.458).

必背考點

A. Relevant Ordinances（Cap. number + section numbers，以 LP 所引為準）

Ordinance Key provisions Core rule
Age of Majority (Related Provisions) Ordinance (Cap.410) s.2(1) A person under 18 lacks capacity to contract; contracts with minors are generally void, subject to exceptions (necessaries / beneficial employment / voidable contracts).
Sale of Goods Ordinance (Cap.26) ('SOGO') s.4(2) Definition of 'necessaries' for minors — goods suitable to the minor's condition in life and actual requirements at the time of sale.
SOGO (Cap.26) ss.14–17 Implied conditions in sale of goods: right to sell (s.14(1)(a)); correspondence with description (s.15(1)); merchantable quality (s.16(2)); bulk corresponding with sample (s.17(2)(a)).
SOGO (Cap.26) s.60(b) At auction, each bid is an offer; acceptance occurs with the fall of the auctioneer's hammer.
SOGO (Cap.26) ss.23(1), 24(1), 25 Nemo dat rule (s.23(1)); market overt exception for goods openly sold in a HK shop/market (s.24(1)); buyer acquires good title from a seller with voidable title not yet avoided, if in good faith without notice (s.25). (Detailed treatment in Ch.3.)
Conveyancing and Property Ordinance (Cap.219) s.3(1) An agreement (or memorandum) for the disposition of land must be in writing and signed to be actionable.
Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Ordinance (Cap.623) ('CRTPO') s.3, s.4(1)(a), s.4(1)(b), s.4(2), s.4(3), s.4(6), s.5, s.6, s.8(2)–(4) Statutory exception to privity (in force 1 Jan 2016). Two enforceability tests: express provision (s.4(1)(a)) or term purporting to confer a benefit (s.4(1)(b), rebuttable under s.4(3)). Third party must be expressly identified (s.4(2)) and need not exist at formation (s.4(6)). Same remedies as contracting parties (s.5); restrictions on rescission/variation where third party has assented or relied (s.6); defences/set-offs available against third party (s.8).
Control of Exemption Clauses Ordinance (Cap.71) ('CECO') s.2(2)(a), s.4(1), s.5(1)(a)–(b) Applies to business liability; covers clauses excluding/limiting liability, rights or remedies. 'Dealing as a consumer' = party not contracting in the course of a business while the other party is (s.4(1)).
CECO (Cap.71) s.7(1), s.7(2) Negligence liability: can NEVER exclude liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence (s.7(1)); other loss/damage from negligence can be excluded only if the term passes the reasonableness test (s.7(2)).
CECO (Cap.71) s.8(1), (2)(a)–(b) Against a party dealing as consumer or on the other's written standard terms: breach of contract liability, or rendering substantially different/no performance, cannot be excluded unless reasonable.
CECO (Cap.71) s.3(1), s.3(4), Sch.2 Reasonableness test: the term must be fair and reasonable to include, judged at the time the contract was MADE (not breach); burden of proof on the party relying on the clause; Sch.2 guidelines (bargaining strength, inducement, knowledge of the term, practicability of conditions, special-order goods); s.3(4) — whether the language was understood (HK bilingual contracts).
CECO (Cap.71) s.9(1), s.10, s.11(1)–(3), s.14 Indemnity clauses against consumers must be reasonable (s.9(1)); manufacturers' guarantees of consumer goods cannot exclude liability for defective goods/negligent manufacture (s.10); SOGO title undertakings (s.14 SOGO) cannot be excluded at all (s.11(1) CECO); SOGO ss.15–17 liabilities excludable in B2B only if reasonable (ss.11(3), 3(2)), never against a consumer (s.11(2)); no evasion via secondary contract (s.14).
Supply of Services (Implied Terms) Ordinance (Cap.457) ('SSITO') ss.5, 6(1), 7(1), 8(1) Implied terms: reasonable care and skill (s.5); performance within a reasonable time if time not agreed (s.6(1)); reasonable charge if price not agreed (s.7(1)); cannot exempt liability to a consumer by contract term (s.8(1)).
Misrepresentation Ordinance (Cap.284) ('MO') s.4 Any term excluding/restricting liability or remedies for misrepresentation (incl. non-reliance clauses) is of no effect unless it satisfies the CECO s.3(1) reasonableness test.
Unconscionable Contracts Ordinance (Cap.458) ('UCO') s.5(1), s.5(2), s.6(1), s.6(2)(a) Only covers consumer contracts for sale of goods/supply of services — NOT land, NOT B2B. Court may refuse to enforce, enforce the remainder, or revise the unconscionable part (s.5(1)); burden on the claimant (s.5(2)); non-exhaustive factors e.g. bargaining strength (s.6(1)); disregard unconscionability from circumstances not reasonably foreseeable at formation (s.6(2)(a)).

B. Leading cases（full citations，按主題分組）

Offer vs invitation to treat / unilateral contracts - Harvey v Facey (1893) AC 552 — a statement of the lowest price is a mere indication of price, not an offer. - Clifton v Palumbo (1944) 2 All ER 497 — vague statement 'I am prepared to offer you my estate…' not sufficiently definite, especially for land. - Partridge v Crittenden (1968) 1 WLR 1204 — advertisements are generally invitations to treat ('limited stock' rationale). - Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co (1893) 1 QB 256 — reward advertisement = unilateral offer to the world; performing the stipulated act = acceptance; £1,000 bank deposit showed intention to be bound; inconvenience of use = consideration. - Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists (Southern) Ltd (1953) 1 QB 401 — goods on self-service shelves are invitations to treat; contract forms at the check-out. - Fisher v Bell (1961) 1 QB 394 — shop-window display with price tag is an invitation to treat. - Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd (1971) 2 QB 163 — automatic/vending machines are standing offers (no negotiation possible); contract forms when money is inserted, before the ticket issues. - Harvela Investments Ltd v Royal Trust Co of Canada (CI) Ltd (1986) AC 207 — an invitation to tender promising to accept the most competitive bid is binding; referential bids impliedly forbidden.

Termination of offers - Byrne & Co v Van Tienhoven (1880) 5 CPD 344 — revocation is effective only when communicated to the offeree before acceptance. - Shuey v US (1875) 92 US 73 — a reward offer to the public can be revoked via the same medium. - Routledge v Grant (1828) 4 Bing 653 — an offer can be revoked before acceptance unless an option contract (supported by consideration) keeps it open. - Ramsgate Victoria Hotel Co Ltd v Montefiore (1866) LR 1 Ex 109 — an offer lapses after a reasonable time.

Acceptance - Hyde v Wrench (1840) 3 Beav 334 — a counter-offer destroys the original offer (mirror-image rule). - Stevenson, Jacques & Co v McLean (1880) 5 QBD 346 — a mere request for information does not destroy the offer. - Butler Machine Tool Co Ltd v Ex-Cell-O Corp (England) Ltd (1979) 1 WLR 401 — battle of forms: the 'last shot' wins; sellers accepted the buyers' counter-offer by returning the signed acknowledgment slip. - Adams v Lindsell (1818) 1 B & Ald 681 — postal rule: posted acceptance is effective on posting. - Holwell Securities Ltd v Hughes (1974) 1 WLR 155 — general rule: acceptance must be communicated; postal rule excluded where receipt is expressly/impliedly required. - Household Fire and Carriage Accident Insurance Co Ltd v Grant (1879) 4 Ex D 216 — postal acceptance effective even if the letter is lost. - R v Clarke (1927) 40 CLR 227 — a person cannot accept an offer in ignorance of it.

Capacity / intention - Proform Sports Management Ltd v Proactive Sports Management Ltd (2006) EWHC 2903 — a minor's contract (Wayne Rooney's agency contract) was voidable, not a contract for necessaries/apprenticeship. - Hart v O'Connor (1985) AC 1000 — mental incapacity makes a contract voidable only if the other party knew of it; otherwise the incapacity is ignored. - Balfour v Balfour (1919) 2 KB 571 — domestic agreements presumed not legally binding. - Jones v Padavatton (1969) 1 WLR 328 — mother–daughter agreement: presumption not rebutted. - Wu Chiu Kuen v Chu Shui Ching (1992) HKCU 29 — HK example where the domestic/social presumption WAS rebutted. - World Food Fair Ltd v Hong Kong Island Development Ltd (2007) 1 HKLRD 498 — 'subject to contract' rebuts (though not conclusively) the commercial presumption.

Consideration - Currie v Misa (1875) LR 10 Ex 153 — classic definition: some right/interest/profit/benefit to one party, or forbearance/detriment/loss/responsibility of the other. - Tweddle v Atkinson (1861) 1 B & S 393 — consideration must move from the promisee; also an authority for privity. - Chappell & Co Ltd v Nestle Co Ltd (1960) AC 87 — consideration must be sufficient (some value, even chocolate wrappers) but need not be adequate. - Roscorla v Thomas (1842) 3 QB 234 — past consideration is no consideration. - Pao On v Lau Yiu Long (1980) AC 614 — three requirements for the 'implied assumpsit' exception (act at promisor's request; understanding of remuneration; enforceability if promised in advance); also: performance of a duty owed to a third party is good consideration. - Lampleigh v Brathwait (1615) Hob 105 — request exception to past consideration. - Collins v Godefroy (1831) 1 B & Ad 950 — performance of an existing duty imposed by law is no consideration. - Glasbrook Brothers Ltd v Glamorgan County Council (1925) AC 270 — doing MORE than a legal duty is good consideration. - Scotson v Pegg (1861) 6 Hurl & N 295 — performance of an existing duty owed to a third party is good consideration. - Stilk v Myrick (1809) 2 Camp 317 — performance of an existing duty owed to the promisor is no consideration (deserting sailors). - Hartley v Ponsonby (1857) 7 E & B 872 — contrast: voyage became so dangerous that remaining crew did more than their existing duty. - Williams v Roffey Bros & Nicholls (Contractors) Ltd (1991) 1 QB 1 — 'practical benefit' exception for promises to pay more (six strict criteria; no economic duress/fraud). - UBC (Construction) Ltd v Sung Foo Kee Ltd (1993) 2 HKLR 207 — HK application of Williams v Roffey. - Pinnel's Case (1602) 5 Co Rep 117a — part-payment of a debt is no consideration for discharging the whole debt; needs fresh consideration (e.g. horse, hawk, robe, or earlier payment). - Foakes v Beer (1884) 9 App Cas 605 — House of Lords confirmed Pinnel's Case; Re Selectmove Ltd (1995) 1 WLR 474 — practical-benefit analysis does NOT apply to promises to accept less (stare decisis).

Promissory estoppel - Central London Property Ltd v High Trees House Ltd (1947) KB 130 — the foundational case; estoppel suspended (not extinguished) the landlord's right to full rent for 1940–1945. - Combe v Combe (1951) 2 KB 215 — estoppel is a shield, not a sword; consideration remains 'a cardinal necessity'. - Tool Metal Manufacturing Co Ltd v Tungsten Electric Co Ltd (1955) 1 WLR 761 — promisor may withdraw the promise on reasonable notice (suspensory effect). - Collier v P & MJ Wright (Holdings) Ltd (2008) 1 WLR 643 — estoppel may extinguish the balance where creditor voluntarily accepted part-payment and debtor relied; NOT yet followed in HK. - Luo Xing Juan v Estate of Hui Shui See (2009) 12 HKCFAR 1 — HK CFA on the inequitability element.

Mistake / privity - Smith v Hughes (1871) LR 6 QB 597 — objective approach; mistake as to quality/collateral matter ≠ mistake as to terms; caveat emptor. - Hartog v Colin and Shields (1939) 3 All ER 566 — if one party knows the other is mistaken as to a term, the contract cannot be enforced against the mistaken party. - Cundy v Lindsay (1878) 3 App Cas 459 — written contract with a rogue impersonating a KNOWN, identifiable firm: void ab initio for mistake as to identity. - King's Norton Metal Co v Edridge Merrett & Co Ltd (1897) 14 TLR 98 — no known existing entity impersonated: mistake only as to attributes (creditworthiness); contract not void. - Ingram v Little (1961) 1 QB 31 — face-to-face presumption rebutted where identity was essential to the contract (not just payment); of questionable authority. - Lewis v Avery (1972) 1 QB 198 — face-to-face presumption NOT rebutted; mistake as to attribute only. - Shogun Finance Ltd v Hudson (2004) 1 AC 919 — the face-to-face presumption is strong; rebutted only in rare cases (e.g. impersonating a family member/personal acquaintance). - Saunders v Anglia Building Society (sub nom Gallie v Lee) (1971) AC 1004 — non est factum: document must be essentially different in substance or kind; signer must also have exercised reasonable care. - Lewis v Clay (1897) 67 LJ QB 224 — non est factum succeeded: signer mistaken as to the CAPACITY in which he signed (thought he was witnessing). - L'Estrange v E Graucob Ltd (1934) 2 KB 394 — a signature binds the signer to unread terms. - Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co Ltd v Selfridge & Co Ltd (1915) AC 847 — privity: only a party to the contract can sue on it. - Beswick v Beswick (1968) AC 58 — widow could not sue personally (no privity) but succeeded as administratrix of the estate. - Shanklin Pier Ltd v Detel Products Ltd (1951) 2 KB 854 — collateral contract as a common law circumvention of privity.

Terms / exemption clauses - Chan Yeuk Yu v Church Body of the Hong Kong Sheng Kung Hui (2001) 1 HKC 621 — 'regal surroundings for the select few' = mere puff (HK). - Heilbut Symons & Co v Buckleton (1913) AC 30 — term vs representation determined objectively by intention; guiding principles are rebuttable. - Dick Bentley Productions Ltd v Harold Smith (Motors) Ltd (1965) 1 WLR 623 — statement by a party with specialist knowledge (car dealer on mileage) = term. - Oscar Chess v Williams (1957) 1 WLR 370 — contrast: private seller without specialist knowledge = representation. - Bannerman v White (1861) 10 CB NS 844 — statement of crucial importance to the buyer (sulphur-free hops) = term. - Curtis v Chemical Cleaning and Dyeing Co (1951) 1 KB 805 — misrepresentation of the extent of an exemption clause defeats reliance on it, despite signature. - C. IRAC application points（落 scenario 嘅分析框架）

  1. Offer vs invitation to treat — Issue: is the communication an offer or an ITT? Rule: definitions + established categories (advertisements/shop displays/websites/tenders/auctions). Application: check the exact wording ('will go to the first 500 replies' → unilateral offer; 'for sale' price tag → ITT); objective intention from the language. Conclusion: state who makes the offer and when the contract forms (e.g. at check-out — Boots).
  2. Counter-offer vs request for information — Rule: mirror-image rule (Hyde v Wrench); even a slight variation destroys the offer; asking about flexibility is not a counter-offer (Stevenson v McLean). Application: compare the reply word-by-word with the offer; in battle-of-forms scenarios, identify the 'last shot' and any acceptance by conduct (Butler Machine Tool).
  3. Revocation — Rule: effective only when communicated before acceptance (Byrne v Van Tienhoven); for unilateral offers to the world, revocation via the same medium suffices (Shuey v US). Application: timeline analysis — date of offer, posting of acceptance, receipt, revocation; check whether the postal rule is excluded ('replies received').
  4. Capacity — Rule: minors void subject to necessaries (s.4(2) SOGO) / beneficial employment / voidable contracts (Cap.410 s.2(1)); mental incapacity voidable only if the other party knew (Hart v O'Connor). Application: classify the contract first (necessaries? employment? interest in land/shares/partnership?).
  5. Intention to create legal relations — Rule: domestic/social → presumed not binding (Balfour v Balfour); commercial → strong presumption of binding, rebuttable e.g. by 'subject to contract'. Application: identify context, then look for rebutting evidence (detrimental reliance, bank deposit as in Carlill).
  6. Consideration — Rule checklist: moved from promisee? sufficient (not adequate)? past (Roscorla v Thomas; Pao On exception)? existing duty (which type — by law / owed to third party / owed to promisor)? part-payment (Pinnel/Foakes; exceptions: composition agreements, third-party payment)? Application: map the promise to one of the categories before concluding enforceability; do not confuse with privity or promissory estoppel.
  7. Promissory estoppel — Rule: (1) clear promise not to insist on strict rights; (2) reliance; (3) inequitable to renege; suspensory only; shield not sword (High Trees; Combe v Combe). Application: estoppel can only DEFEND; if the promisee is the plaintiff claiming extra payment, estoppel fails — look to Williams v Roffey consideration instead.
  8. Exemption clauses — Three-step analysis: (1) incorporation (signature — L'Estrange, subject to misrepresentation — Curtis; reasonable notice before/at formation — Parker, Olley, Thornton; higher notice for onerous terms — Interfoto; consistent course of dealings — McCutcheon); (2) construction — does the wording cover the liability (contra proferentem)?; (3) statutory validity — CECO: death/PI negligence can never be excluded (s.7(1)); other negligence loss needs reasonableness (s.7(2)); consumer/standard-terms breach liability needs reasonableness (s.8); SOGO implied undertakings in consumer contracts cannot be excluded (s.11(2)); reasonableness judged at formation, burden on the party relying (s.3(1), Sch.2).
  9. Third-party rights — Rule: privity burden/benefit rules (Dunlop v Selfridge); CRTPO s.4 two tests + s.4(2) identification + s.4(3) rebuttal. Application: check first whether the contract expressly excludes CRTPO ('A person who is not a party… shall have no right…'), then whether the third party is identified by name/class/description.
  10. UCO (Cap.458) — Rule: consumer goods/services only; unconscionability is a HIGH threshold (higher than 'unfairness'); court's three powers under s.5(1). Application: raise UCO only after CECO/MO/SSITO fail; expect rare success (LRC: 'extreme cases, very rare').

Invitation to treat 邀請出價——唔係 offer，對方回應先係 offer A shop-window display of goods with a price tag is merely an invitation to treat (Fisher v Bell (1961) 1 QB 394).
Unilateral contract 單方合約——一方出承諾，另一方以行動接受 A reward advertisement constitutes a unilateral offer which is accepted by performance of the stipulated act (Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co (1893) 1 QB 256).
Mirror-image rule 照鏡規則——acceptance 必須同 offer 完全一樣 Any variation of the offer's terms constitutes a counter-offer which destroys the original offer (Hyde v Wrench (1840) 3 Beav 334).
Consideration 約因——用嚟換對方承諾嘅代價，必須有價值但唔需要等值 Consideration must be sufficient but need not be adequate (Chappell & Co Ltd v Nestle Co Ltd (1960) AC 87).
Past consideration 過去約因——承諾之前已做嘅行為，原則上唔算數 A promise made after the act has been performed is supported only by past consideration and is unenforceable (Roscorla v Thomas (1842) 3 QB 234).
Promissory estoppel 承諾性禁止反言——只可防守、只暫停權利 Promissory estoppel operates as a shield, not a sword, and is suspensory rather than extinctive (Combe v Combe (1951) 2 KB 215).
Privity of contract 合約相對性——非合約方無權利無義務 Under the benefit rule, a third party cannot enforce a term of a contract to which he is not privy, subject to the CRTPO (Cap.623).
Reasonableness test 合理性測試——以簽約當刻衡量，舉證責任喺依賴條款一方 Under s.3(1) of the CECO (Cap.71), the clause must be fair and reasonable to include having regard to circumstances known or contemplated when the contract was made.
