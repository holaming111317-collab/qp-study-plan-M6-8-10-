**Q1.** Golden Hour Jewellery Ltd displays a vintage Rolex watch in its shop window in Tsim Sha Tsui with a price tag of HK$88,000. Marcus sees the display, walks in, and tells the shop assistant: "I accept your offer — here is HK$88,000 in cash." The shop manager refuses to sell, saying the price tag was printed with the wrong price. Marcus insists the shop is contractually bound to sell him the watch at HK$88,000.

Which statement BEST describes the legal position?

A. The shop is bound: the window display constituted a unilateral offer to the world, which Marcus accepted by tendering the price (Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co (1893) 1 QB 256).
B. The shop is not bound: the window display is an invitation to treat, so Marcus's tender of HK$88,000 is merely an offer which the shop is free to reject (Fisher v Bell (1961) 1 QB 394).
C. The shop is bound: the window display is a standing offer because it cannot be negotiated with, like an automatic machine (Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd (1971) 2 QB 163).
D. The shop is not bound, but only because the marked price was a mistake, rendering any contract void for unilateral mistake as to terms.

**Q3.** Karen offers to sell her ceramic studio equipment to Daniel for HK$350,000, the offer to remain open for 14 days. On day 3, Daniel replies by email: "Thanks — would you consider HK$320,000, payable in six monthly instalments?" Karen does not respond. On day 9, Daniel emails again: "I accept your original offer of HK$350,000 in full." Karen now refuses to sell, arguing that Daniel destroyed her offer with his day-3 email and that she is free to sell to another buyer at a higher price.

Which statement BEST describes the legal position?

A. No contract exists: Daniel's day-3 email was a counter-offer which destroyed Karen's original offer, so it could no longer be accepted (Hyde v Wrench (1840) 3 Beav 334).
B. A contract exists: Daniel's day-3 email was a mere request for information which did not destroy the offer, and his day-9 email was a valid acceptance within the 14-day period (Stevenson, Jacques & Co v McLean (1880) 5 QBD 346).
C. No contract exists: Karen's offer had lapsed after a reasonable time given the volatile market price of ceramic equipment (Ramsgate Victoria Hotel Co Ltd v Montefiore (1866) LR 1 Ex 109).
D. A contract exists: even if Daniel's day-3 email was a counter-offer, Karen's silence amounted to acceptance by conduct of that counter-offer.

**Q4.** BrightBuild Ltd is the main contractor for the renovation of a shopping arcade in Mong Kok under a contract that imposes a HK$500,000 penalty for late completion. BrightBuild subcontracts the electrical work to Sparky Ltd for HK$1,200,000, due in 10 weeks. In week 7, Sparky falls seriously behind schedule because of financial difficulties and an under-resourced workforce. Fearing the penalty, BrightBuild promises Sparky an additional HK$150,000 if it completes the electrical work on time as originally agreed. Sparky completes on time. BrightBuild refuses to pay the HK$150,000, arguing that Sparky provided no consideration because it merely did what it was already contractually bound to do. There is no evidence of fraud or economic duress by Sparky.

Which statement BEST describes whether Sparky can enforce the promise of the extra HK$150,000?

A. Sparky cannot enforce the promise: performance of an existing contractual duty owed to the promisor can never constitute consideration (Stilk v Myrick (1809) 2 Camp 317).
B. Sparky can enforce the promise: BrightBuild obtained a practical benefit — avoiding the HK$500,000 late-completion penalty and the trouble of engaging a replacement subcontractor — and the strict criteria are satisfied (Williams v Roffey Bros & Nicholls (Contractors) Ltd (1991) 1 QB 1).
C. Sparky cannot enforce the promise: a promise to pay more in return for part performance is unenforceable unless supported by fresh consideration such as early payment or a chattel (Pinnel's Case (1602) 5 Co Rep 117a).
D. Sparky can enforce the promise by relying on promissory estoppel as a cause of action, since it relied on BrightBuild's promise by completing the work (Central London Property Ltd v High Trees House Ltd (1947) KB 130).

**Q5.** Ms. Wong signs up for a one-year membership at FitZone Gym in Causeway Bay. The membership is on FitZone's standard printed terms, which she signs without reading. Clause 12 states: "FitZone Gym Ltd, its employees and agents accept no liability whatsoever for any injury, loss or damage to members or their property, howsoever caused, including the negligence of FitZone Gym Ltd, its employees or agents." Three months later, a treadmill belt snaps because FitZone's staff negligently failed to maintain the equipment. Ms. Wong suffers a fractured wrist, and her smartphone (worth HK$9,000), stored in a gym locker, is smashed in the fall. FitZone relies on Clause 12 to refuse all compensation.

Which statement BEST describes the validity of Clause 12?

A. Clause 12 is fully effective for both claims: Ms. Wong signed the contract and is therefore bound by all its terms, whether or not she read them (L'Estrange v E Graucob Ltd (1934) 2 KB 394).
B. Clause 12 is entirely ineffective: no exemption clause can ever exclude liability for negligence of any kind under the Control of Exemption Clauses Ordinance (Cap.71).
C. Clause 12 cannot exclude FitZone's liability for Ms. Wong's fractured wrist because liability for personal injury resulting from negligence can never be excluded (s.7(1) of the CECO (Cap.71)); however, it may exclude liability for the damaged smartphone if FitZone proves the clause satisfies the reasonableness test in s.3(1) of the CECO (s.7(2) of the CECO).
D. Clause 12 is governed exclusively by the Unconscionable Contracts Ordinance (Cap.458), under which the court may revise the clause so as to avoid an unconscionable result (s.5(1) of the UCO).
