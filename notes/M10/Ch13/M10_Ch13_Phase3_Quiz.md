**Q3.** Marble Design Ltd was wound up by the court. The winding-up petition was presented on 1 June, and the winding-up order was made on 15 August. The liquidator discovers the following transactions, both entered into while Marble Design Ltd was already insolvent:

- (i) On 1 March, the company repaid in full an unsecured loan of HK$400,000 owed to the wife of its managing director, although the loan was not yet due;
- (ii) On 1 April, the company granted its bank a floating charge over all its assets as security for a pre-existing, previously unsecured overdraft, in response to the bank's threat to demand immediate repayment. No new money was advanced.

Which of the following is the **best** analysis of the liquidator's position?

A. Neither transaction can be challenged, because both were entered into more than 21 days before the winding-up order was made.
B. Only transaction (i) can be challenged as an unfair preference, because the two-year look-back period applies to associates, whereas the six-month period for transaction (ii) has expired.
C. Only transaction (ii) can be challenged, because repayment of a loan to a director's spouse is a legitimate discharge of the company's liability and cannot be a preference.
D. Both transactions can potentially be challenged as unfair preferences: the winding-up is deemed to have commenced on 1 June (presentation of the petition), transaction (ii) falls within the six-month period, and transaction (i) falls within the extended two-year period because the managing director's wife is an associate.

**Q4.** Jade Properties Ltd granted Bank K a debenture secured by a charge over its office building. On default, Bank K privately appointed Raymond, an accountant, as receiver. Raymond promptly sold the building by private treaty to a company owned by Bank K's holding company for HK$20 million, without obtaining any independent valuation, without advertising the sale, and despite a neighbouring owner having earlier indicated to Raymond that he was prepared to bid HK$26 million. The building's market value was approximately HK$25 million. Jade Properties Ltd now complains.

Which of the following is the **best** analysis of Raymond's position?

A. Raymond is not in breach of any duty, because a privately appointed receiver owes duties only to his appointor, Bank K, and the sale discharged the secured debt.
B. Raymond is likely in breach of his duties: although he may sell to a party connected with the appointor, he must act in good faith and take reasonable precautions to obtain the best price reasonably obtainable, including taking expert advice on the method of sale — which he failed to do by ignoring the indicated higher bidder.
C. Raymond is in breach of his fiduciary duty to Jade Properties Ltd, because a receiver, as the company's agent, owes full fiduciary duties to the company and must subordinate the appointor's interests to the company's interests.
D. Raymond is in breach of duty only if Jade Properties Ltd can prove that he acted with actual dishonesty or fraudulent intent.

**Q5.** Golden Wok Holdings Ltd operated a chain of restaurants. In September, it was in severe cash flow difficulty and owed several suppliers substantial sums. Its director, Daniel, signed a letter of intent with a private equity fund which, after weeks of negotiation, indicated it would inject HK$30 million. Genuinely relying on the imminent injection, Daniel caused the company to accept further supplies on credit in October. The fund then abruptly withdrew from the deal for its own commercial reasons, the injection never materialised, and the company was wound up in December, unable to pay the suppliers. The liquidator commences fraudulent trading proceedings against Daniel under s.275(1) of the Companies (Winding Up and Miscellaneous Provisions) Ordinance (Cap.32).

Which of the following is the **best** analysis of Daniel's liability?

A. Daniel is liable for fraudulent trading, because he continued to incur credit when there was no reasonable prospect of the suppliers ever being paid.
B. Daniel is liable for fraudulent trading, because s.275(1) imposes strict liability on directors who cause an insolvent company to incur further debts.
C. Daniel is not liable for fraudulent trading, because liability requires actual dishonesty measured against the standards of reasonable and honest people; his honest belief that the fund injection would materialise is inconsistent with fraud, even if the objective likelihood of survival was low.
D. Daniel is not liable for fraudulent trading because the suppliers could have protected themselves by demanding cash on delivery; their failure to do so breaks the chain of liability.
